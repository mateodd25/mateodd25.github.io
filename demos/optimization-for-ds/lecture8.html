Gauss–Newton and damping

Linearize the residual, then solve a least-squares problem. See why this can be extremely fast near a solution, why a full step can fail, and how Levenberg–Morrison–Marquardt (LMM) controls the step.

1When the linear model asks for too much

Use the example from the notes: r(w) = w² − 1. Near zero, its derivative is tiny. Canceling the linearized residual can require an enormous step, even though the two solutions are close by.

The curvature we keep and the curvature we drop

The full Hessian is f″(w) = 6w² − 2. Gauss–Newton retains J(w)² = 4w² and drops r(w)r″(w) = 2(w² − 1). The dropped term vanishes at w = ±1. At w = 0, the true objective has negative curvature, but its GN model is flat. The inverse formula is undefined there; the minimum-norm convention would choose a zero step. Positive damping also gives a zero step, so damping alone does not escape this local maximum.

2Fast local convergence, and the cost of damping

Start all three methods at the same point on f(w) = ½(w² − 1)². Fixed damping leaves a linear error term. Damping proportional to the residual vanishes near a root and recovers quadratic convergence.

Residual LMM uses λₖ = c |wₖ² − 1|.

Connect the curves to the theorem

For w > 0, set e = w − 1. The exact GN recursion is e₊ = e²/(2w). Thus e₊/e² tends to ½. For LMM, e₊ = [λe + 2we²]/(4w² + λ). A fixed λ > 0 gives an asymptotic error ratio λ/(4 + λ).

With λ = c |r(w)| and r(w) = (w − 1)(w + 1), damping is of the same order as the parameter error near a root. Both terms in the LMM recursion are then quadratic. The default c = 0.5 avoids an accidental higher-order cancellation that occurs for c = 1 when approaching 1 from below.

All methods take full steps with no line search. This is a local convergence illustration: a distant starting point can give a large increase in the objective. The computation stops at a parameter error of 10⁻¹⁴ or after 80 steps; numerical and singularity stops are reported explicitly.

Data, algorithms, and local minima

The 81 observations are generated from yᵢ = sin(2.4tᵢ + 0.4) + noiseᵢ at evenly spaced tᵢ in [0,10].

A fixed seed gives bounded, centered noise; the slider sets its maximum absolute value. These are synthetic observations. With noise, the generating parameters need not minimize the sample loss.

In the noiseless sinusoidal problem, the best loss is zero. The wrong-frequency preset approaches a positive-loss local minimum near (3.2591, −0.7311), with f ≈ 0.39856. Its full Hessian has positive eigenvalues, approximately 0.0644 and 7.1577. Thus the plateau is a bad local minimum, not just slow progress. The different-basins preset sends GN to another local minimum with f ≈ 0.37155. These outcomes use the preset damping values; changing them can change the destination.

All methods use the same residuals and starting point. GN solves (JᵀJ)p = −Jᵀr; fixed LMM adds λI; residual LMM uses λₖ = c‖rₖ‖. We take the full step wₖ₊₁ = wₖ + p. There is no hidden line search, step clipping, constraint, or retry with increased damping. The parameter sliders restrict only the starting point. Damping can stabilize steps, but does not guarantee finding the best basin.

The small linear systems are solved in double precision. Stop when ‖r‖ ≤ 10⁻¹², ‖∇f‖ ≤ 10⁻¹⁰, or after 200 full steps. A singular or numerically ill-conditioned system, stagnation, or an unsafe numerical evaluation is reported separately. Small gradient alone is not a certificate of a global minimum. The numerical table and CSV retain the actual iterates. Noisy fits and positive-loss local minima are not covered by the zero-residual quadratic convergence theorem.
